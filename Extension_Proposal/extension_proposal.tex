{\color{MSSGray}\small Did financial intermediation transform rich saving into middle-class mortgage debt?}

\compactheading{Motivation and research question}

Mian, Straub, and Sufi (MSS) document three connected changes. Figure 1 shows
the indirectly held share of household financial assets rising from about 45\%
in 1960 to 70\% in 2007. Figure 6 shows the top 1\% saving rate rising from 43\%
to 54\% after 1982 while the rest falls from 14\% to 7\%. Figure 8 unveils a
widening top-lender/middle-borrower position before 2008. Yet the ownership
operator is payoff-blind: it traces who owns a dollar claim, not whether
intermediation reduced its risk, relaxed credit supply, weakened screening, or
shifted losses through capital and guarantees.

\noindent\fcolorbox{MSSOrange}{MSSPale}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.955\textwidth}
\textbf{Question.} Did pooling, securitization, and secondary-market liquidity
convert the saving glut of the rich into cheaper and more abundant mortgages,
thereby lowering middle-wealth households' saving rates; and who ultimately
bore the resulting state-contingent losses?
\end{minipage}}

\compactheading{A borrower--saver--intermediary model}

Let mortgage $i$ pay $R_{it}=\bar R_t-\eta_t-\varepsilon_{it}$, with aggregate
risk $\sigma^2_{A,t}$ and idiosyncratic risk $\sigma^2_{I,t}$. Financialization
$\phi_t$ permits a pool of $N(\phi_t)$ loans, so

\begin{equation*}
 \operatorname{Var}(\bar R_{N,t})
 =\sigma^2_{A,t}+\frac{\sigma^2_{I,t}}{N(\phi_t)},
 \qquad
 r^L_t-r^f_t
 =c(\phi_t)+\gamma_t\operatorname{Var}(\bar R_{N,t})
  +\mu(\phi_t,e_t,k_t,g_t).
\end{equation*}

Pooling removes idiosyncratic risk, not the aggregate housing shock. The loan
spread also contains funding cost $c$, screening $e$, loss-absorbing capital
$k$, guarantees $g$, and an agency/opacity wedge $\mu$. Hence
$\partial(r^L-r^f)/\partial\phi$ is ambiguous: intermediation can reduce funding
and diversification costs while weakening screening or reallocating capital.

A middle household chooses consumption, housing, and mortgage debt subject to
$b\leq\lambda(\phi_t,e_t)q_t h$. Its debt choice maps directly into Figure 6:

\begin{equation*}
 s^{L,m}_{gt}=-\frac{L^m_{gt}-(1-WD^m_{gt})L^m_{g,t-1}}{Z^d_{gt}},
\end{equation*}

where $WD^m_{gt}$ is the write-down rate and $Z^d_{gt}$ is group disposable
income including attributable corporate income. Rich savers choose direct and
intermediated claims; market clearing links their saving to mortgage funding,
but ownership alone is not a causal loan-supply equation.

\compactheading{The methodological increment: a separate loss operator}

MSS compute nominal ultimate ownership as
$\Omega_t=(I-Q_t)^{-1}H_t$. For state $\omega$, instead let $P_t(\omega)$ pass
marginal losses between intermediaries and $C_t(\omega)$ pass them to terminal
owners after capital, seniority, and guarantees. For primary losses
$\ell_t^0(\omega)$,

\begin{equation*}
 \ell_t^O(\omega)=\ell_t^0(\omega)
 (I-P_t(\omega))^{-1}C_t(\omega),
 \qquad
 P_t\mathbf 1+C_t\mathbf 1+a_t=\mathbf 1,
\end{equation*}

where $a_t$ is intermediary-buffer absorption. This local, piecewise-linear
map keeps three objects distinct: face-value ownership, ex ante expected risk,
and realized loss incidence. Large shocks require an explicit nonlinear
waterfall as buffers are exhausted.

\compactheading{Relation and novelty}

The generic links from securitization to mortgage supply and from inequality
to household debt are well studied. The candidate contribution is narrower:
\emph{jointly discipline distributional saving, ultimate claim ownership, and
wealth-group loss incidence, then quantify the intermediation counterfactual}.
It opens the intermediation block in \emph{Indebted Demand} and answers MSS's
call to study why excess saving financed leverage rather than investment.

\resulttag{Interpretation.}
Mortgages dominate the boom-era debt build-up, the debt-related saving drag,
and the widening top-lender/middle-borrower split. For the bottom 99\%, the
mortgage saving contribution then collapses from
$\ExtensionBottomNinetyNineMortgageBoom$ in 1998--2007 to
$\ExtensionBottomNinetyNineMortgagePost$ in 2008--2016, so the model should
target the pre-crisis regime. This is accounting incidence, not a causal
financialization estimate.

\compactheading{Empirical and quantitative sequence}

\textbf{1. Close the MSS measurement identity.}
Use the exact Figure 6 group-income denominator and percentile bins; require
mortgage plus consumer credit to recover total debt saving and all asset classes
to recover the complete saving rate.

\textbf{2. Identify the intermediation channel.}
Measure securitization, loan sales, GSE/private-label shares, originator funding
and capital, retention, and guarantees separately. Estimate approval, rate,
amount, and performance responses using conforming-loan eligibility or
predetermined lender exposure. Test cutoff manipulation, borrower sorting, and
the lender-exposure exclusion restriction.

\textbf{3. Estimate the model and unveil losses.}
Match Figures 1, 6, and 8; mortgage spreads and originations; intermediary
funding/capital; and default/loss severity. Simulate nominal ownership and
state-contingent losses, enforcing loss conservation across security holders,
intermediary capital, government guarantees, and the rest of the world.

\textbf{4. Run the counterfactual.}
Holding 2007 incomes, collateral, and borrower fundamentals fixed, replace the
2007 intermediation/risk-bearing structure with its 1982 counterpart. Measure
the implied change in middle mortgage debt and Figure 6 saving, then separate
intermediation from inequality, collateral, and agency/guarantee wedges.

pipeline with HMDA, Call Reports, conforming limits, GSE performance, SCF/DFA,
and house prices.

\compactheading{Evidentiary boundary}

The proposal does not assume that financialization caused top saving or that
more credit reduced welfare. Pooling can lower idiosyncratic risk while leaving
aggregate risk intact; securitization may relax funding constraints while
weakening screening. The contribution is to estimate these offsetting forces
and distinguish who owns the claim from who bears the loss.
