# From Creative Destruction to Household Debt: Globalization, Technology, Financialization, and the Saving Glut of the Rich

## Executive assessment

The two ideas can be integrated into a coherent research agenda, but they should be sharpened in three important ways.

First, the project should not begin by assuming that technological change has universally accelerated firm entry and exit throughout the U.S. economy. Economic theory supports a connection between technological progress, obsolescence, entry, and firm displacement, and there is evidence of such mechanisms in particular industries. However, aggregate U.S. business dynamism and high-growth startup activity have generally declined in recent decades. The model should therefore allow technological change to generate faster obsolescence in exposed sectors, but whether this force has strengthened over time should be treated as an empirical hypothesis rather than an established fact.

Second, highly innovative firms should not all be modeled as relying primarily on bank debt. Ordinary startups often use external debt, but frontier technology firms typically hold substantial intangible capital, which is difficult to pledge as collateral. They consequently rely more heavily on founder wealth, venture capital, outside equity, convertible claims, and retained earnings. The shift toward intangible production may therefore simultaneously increase equity-financed entrepreneurial wealth and redirect traditional bank lending toward collateralizable household assets, especially mortgages.

Third, higher stock-market valuations or entrepreneurial returns do not automatically explain the saving-rate result in Mian, Straub, and Sufi. Their net-saving measure deliberately separates active saving from capital gains. To explain the top saving rate, the model must generate higher retained corporate earnings, higher disposable income that is not consumed, or both—not merely rising company valuations.

The strongest integrated mechanism is therefore:

[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{global market expansion and scalable technology}\
&\qquad\Downarrow\
&\text{more concentrated entrepreneurial profits and corporate saving}\
&\qquad\Downarrow\
&\text{greater saving by wealthy business owners and investors}\
&\qquad\Downarrow\
&\text{financial intermediation and securitization}\
&\qquad\Downarrow\
&\text{credit reallocation toward mortgages and household debt}\
&\qquad\Downarrow\
&\text{higher house prices, middle-class leverage, and lower active saving}.
\end{aligned}
}
]

This would directly extend Mian, Straub, and Sufi. Their paper documents that top saving surged after the 1980s and ultimately financed middle-class household borrowing before 2008 and federal debt afterward, but it does not provide a structural account of the technological and financial forces that produced this allocation. The authors explicitly identify the incentives and constraints governing the financial allocation of excess saving as an important subject for future research.

The current reconstruction also strengthens the motivation: the decline in upper-middle saving persists even when the same saving-rate points are plotted against approximate real wealth levels rather than percentile ranks. At the reconstructed 80th percentile, for example, real wealth rises while the saving rate falls substantially. The result is descriptive rather than causal, but it weakens the strongest version of the claim that the pattern is only a consequence of households moving along a stable wealth-saving schedule.

The personal note correctly treats entrepreneurship, technology, and borrowing mechanisms as hypotheses rather than conclusions of the replication.

# Part I. Sharpening the technology and globalization hypothesis

## 1. Four concepts that should not be conflated

The original intuition combines several changes that are economically distinct:

[
\lambda_t
=========

\text{arrival rate of new technologies},
]

[
\delta_t
========

\text{obsolescence or displacement hazard faced by incumbents},
]

[
G_t
===

\text{addressable market size created by globalization and digital scalability},
]

and

[
\phi_t
======

\text{efficiency and scope of financial intermediation}.
]

These variables need not move together.

A higher arrival rate of technological opportunities can stimulate entry. But a higher displacement hazard shortens the expected life of a successful project and may discourage investment unless the potential prize also increases.

A larger global market raises the payoff from becoming a technological leader but can simultaneously make competition more severe for lagging firms.

More efficient financial intermediation can increase credit supply, but it does not necessarily direct that credit toward innovative firms.

Separating these margins is central to a credible model.

---

## 2. Does faster technological change cause more firm birth and death?

### What theory supports

There is a well-developed theoretical foundation for modeling entry, innovation, displacement, and exit.

Klette and Kortum construct a general-equilibrium model in which heterogeneous firms innovate, acquire and lose product lines, and enter and exit over time. Acemoglu and Cao distinguish radical innovation by entrants from incremental innovation by incumbents. Earlier industry-life-cycle models show that innovation opportunities induce entry while failure to adopt a superior technology can force firms to leave.

There is also direct support for the more specific proposition that rapid technological progress can accelerate industry shakeouts. Jovanovic and Tse find that shakeouts tend to occur earlier in industries where technological progress is faster. Trade liberalization can also accelerate creative destruction by enlarging the competitive field and facilitating the international movement of technology.

So a model with a technology-dependent firm displacement hazard is entirely defensible:

[
\Pr(\text{firm exit}_{i,t+1})
=============================

\delta(q_{it},\lambda_t,x_{it}),
]

where (q_{it}) is the firm's current technological quality and (x_{it}) is its innovation effort.

### What the aggregate evidence does not support

It would nevertheless be inaccurate to assume that economy-wide U.S. firm turnover has simply accelerated since the early 1980s. Business entry, job reallocation, and the prevalence of high-growth young firms have generally declined, particularly after 2000. The responsiveness of employment growth to firm-level productivity has also weakened.

Recent evidence also shows that different technologies can have opposite effects. Greater exposure to cloud computing has been associated with more entry and exit in affected Chinese industries, whereas AI adoption reduced exit and benefited larger firms disproportionately.

Nor should rapid advances at the technological frontier be equated with immediate economy-wide diffusion. A recent study finds that employment associated with major technologies can take decades to disperse geographically.

### Appropriate modeling conclusion

The model should therefore not impose

[
\lambda_t\uparrow
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{aggregate entry and exit}\uparrow
]

as an unconditional long-run fact.

A better formulation is:

[
\frac{\partial \delta_{jt}}
{\partial \lambda_{jt}}>0
]

in technology-intensive sector (j), while aggregate entry and exit remain equilibrium outcomes affected by financing constraints, incumbent acquisitions, market concentration, demographic trends, and the difficulty of developing new ideas.

The empirical project should test whether particular industries exposed to shorter technology cycles exhibit greater displacement, financing needs, and return dispersion—not claim that all firms face faster turnover.

---

## 3. Globalization and scalability are the stronger part of the hypothesis

The globalization mechanism has considerably stronger empirical support.

A larger addressable market increases the prize for successful innovation:

[
V_{it}
======

G_t\pi(q_{it})-\text{costs},
]

where (G_t) measures effective market scale.

If profits are convex in technological quality,

[
\frac{\partial^2 V}
{\partial G,\partial q}>0,
]

then market expansion disproportionately benefits the most productive or technologically advanced firms.

This mechanism closely resembles the superstar-firm literature. Autor and coauthors show that if globalization or technology reallocates sales toward the most productive firms, industries become more concentrated and an increasing share of economic activity is controlled by high-markup firms.

Firm-level export-demand evidence is also highly relevant. Exogenous growth in foreign demand causes firms to innovate more, but the patent response is concentrated among firms that were already relatively productive. The response takes several years, suggesting that larger markets increase both the scale and the incentive for innovation rather than producing instantaneous technological adjustment.

Innovation is also positively associated with top-income inequality, and entrant innovation is associated with mobility into the upper tail.

Thus the strongest firm-side mechanism is not necessarily:

> Technology changes faster, so more firms are constantly born and die.

It is:

> **Globalization and digital scalability increase the payoff to successful innovation, producing more skewed firm outcomes, greater profit concentration, and exceptionally large wealth gains for the owners of successful firms.**

This can coexist with declining aggregate startup rates.

---

## 4. The effect of competition on innovation is not unambiguously positive

A credible model also needs to avoid assuming that more competition always produces more innovation.

The literature finds an inverted-U relationship: competition can encourage neck-and-neck firms to innovate in order to escape competition, but it can discourage lagging firms whose probability of catching up is low.

Likewise, foreign competition can either increase or reduce innovation depending on firm productivity and market structure. Trade may enlarge markets and increase the value of successful innovation, while simultaneously reducing expected profits or eliminating less productive firms.

The model should therefore permit:

[
\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial G}>0
]

for productive leaders and promising entrants, but

[
\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial G}\leq 0
]

for sufficiently far-behind firms.

That heterogeneity is essential for generating both superstar firms and firm exit.

# Part II. Sharpening the entrepreneurial-finance mechanism

## 5. New firms do need external finance—but not all use the same type

The claim that new firms require external funding is strongly supported.

Evidence from the Kauffman Firm Survey shows that ordinary new firms rely substantially on formal external debt, including bank finance.

However, highly innovative firms present a different financing problem. New and small R&D-intensive firms face unusually high costs of capital, and venture capital mitigates only part of this financing gap. Large established firms, by contrast, tend to finance innovation internally through cash flows and retained earnings.

This distinction suggests three firm types:

1. **Ordinary small businesses**, which frequently depend on bank loans and the owner's personal balance sheet.
2. **Frontier innovative startups**, which rely more on founder equity, venture capital, convertible claims, and specialized finance.
3. **Large incumbents**, which can finance innovation through retained earnings, corporate cash, bond markets, or acquisitions.

The startup-financing equation should be

[
I_{it}
======

n_{it}
+
b_{it}
+
e_{it},
]

where (n_{it}) is founder net worth, (b_{it}) is debt, and (e_{it}) is outside equity.

The debt constraint should depend on asset pledgeability:

[
b_{it}
\leq
\theta_T K^T_{it}
+
\theta_I K^I_{it},
\qquad
\theta_I<\theta_T,
]

where (K^T) is tangible capital and (K^I) is intangible capital.

A technology firm whose primary assets are software, data, organizational knowledge, or human capital may have a high expected value but little collateral that a conventional bank can recover after default.

---

## 6. Intangible capital provides the key bridge between the two ideas

This is possibly the most promising connection in the entire project.

The transition toward a knowledge economy has made production increasingly dependent on intangible capital. Federal Reserve research finds that banks more exposed to the transition from tangible to intangible production curtailed commercial lending and reallocated their portfolios toward assets such as mortgages. The estimated rise in intangible capital since the mid-1980s accounts for roughly 30% of the secular decline in commercial lending's share of bank portfolios.

This creates the following chain:

[
\text{technology becomes more intangible}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{innovative business assets become less pledgeable}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{innovative firms rely more on equity, VC, and retained earnings}
]

while

[
\text{banks reallocate toward mortgages and other collateralized loans}.
]

This mechanism can reconcile two otherwise puzzling facts:

* technological and entrepreneurial wealth creation can become increasingly important at the top;
* the aggregate financial system can nevertheless direct a large share of additional saving toward housing and household debt rather than broad productive investment.

That is closely aligned with Mian, Straub, and Sufi's central result. They find that the top saving glut financed middle-class debt and government deficits rather than producing a comparable increase in productive capital formation.

---

## 7. Rich saving does not automatically fund small innovative firms

Another recent result is particularly important for the project.

High-income households save relatively more in stocks and bonds and relatively less in bank deposits. Because small firms are more bank-dependent than large firms, rising top income shares can increase their relative financing cost rather than automatically supplying them with more capital. A 2026 study estimates that this channel explains part of the decline in small-firm employment since 1980.

This provides an important correction to the original intuition.

It is not enough for rich households to save more. The relevant question is:

[
\boxed{
\text{In what instruments do they save, and which borrowers can issue those instruments?}
}
]

A wealthy household may be willing to hold:

* public equity;
* corporate bonds;
* private-equity or venture-capital interests;
* mutual funds;
* mortgage-backed securities;
* government securities.

But an ordinary startup may need a relationship bank loan that cannot readily be transformed into one of these marketable securities.

The model therefore needs **segmented finance**, not a single frictionless pool of loanable funds.

---

## 8. Venture capital is important but narrow

Venture capital can stimulate innovation disproportionately relative to its aggregate size. But VC financing is concentrated in particular sectors, geographical locations, firm types, and potential exit paths.

Dominant incumbent acquisitions can also reduce startup entry and financing incentives in some markets. The theoretical “kill zone” mechanism shows how the prospect of rapid acquisition by a dominant platform can reduce complementary investment and discourage venture financing of independent entrants.

Therefore the project should not imply:

[
\text{top saving}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{all promising startups are easily financed}.
]

Instead:

[
\text{top saving}\uparrow
]

can coexist with

[
\text{abundant finance for a narrow set of scalable firms}
]

and

[
\text{persistent financing constraints for many ordinary or intangible-intensive entrants}.
]

That asymmetry may itself contribute to concentration.

---

# Part III. Sharpening the heterogeneous-return mechanism

## 9. Why wealthy households may capture more of the technological upside

The claim that returns differ systematically across households has empirical support.

Administrative data show substantial and persistent heterogeneity in returns to wealth even within asset classes, and average returns are positively correlated with wealth.

Rich households also hold portfolios disproportionately concentrated in risky assets and private businesses. But wealthy entrepreneurs are often highly exposed to their own firms rather than being perfectly diversified.

The model should therefore distinguish two kinds of wealthy households:

### Entrepreneur-owners

These households hold concentrated positions in their own businesses. Their wealth is highly exposed to firm-specific risk, but successful outcomes can create extremely large returns.

### Diversified investors

These households have sufficient wealth to pay fixed participation costs, hire professional managers, invest in funds, and spread exposures across many companies and asset classes.

For diversified investors, increasing the number of independent investments (N) can reduce idiosyncratic variance:

[
Var\left(
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_i
\right)
\downarrow
\quad\text{as }N\uparrow.
]

But it does not eliminate:

* aggregate technology risk;
* market crashes;
* correlated startup failures;
* liquidity risk;
* housing-system risk;
* intermediary and agency risk.

The model should not assume that financialization transforms risky claims into virtually riskless high-return assets. It transforms and reallocates risk; it does not make aggregate risk disappear.

---

## 10. Why middle-wealth households may not capture the same returns

Several mechanisms can limit middle-class participation in high-return assets:

[
\text{fixed participation costs},
]

[
\text{limited liquid wealth},
]

[
\text{housing and mortgage concentration},
]

[
\text{risk and ambiguity aversion},
]

[
\text{lack of access to private markets},
]

and

[
\text{labor income risk correlated with business conditions}.
]

Mortgage debt is especially relevant. Households with mortgage debt are less likely to hold stocks and bonds than otherwise similar households, partly because repaying debt offers a comparatively attractive risk-free return and because the household balance sheet is already leveraged.

Thus the integrated mechanism can include a portfolio crowd-out channel:

[
\text{mortgage burden}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{liquid financial saving}\downarrow
\Rightarrow
\text{risky-asset participation}\downarrow.
]

This can widen wealth inequality even if financial markets technically become more accessible.

The model should nevertheless permit lower-wealth households to benefit from innovation through wages, employment, pensions, public-equity funds, and lower consumer prices. The proposition that they receive *no* benefit should be tested rather than assumed.

# Part IV. The household-credit and housing-price proposal

## 11. Core hypothesis

The second proposed project can be formulated as:

> **Did financial innovation expand mortgage credit in a way that raised housing prices, induced middle-wealth households to take on larger debts, and reduced their saving conditional on real wealth?**

The basic feedback is

[
\phi_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{mortgage credit supply}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
P^H_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{required mortgage size}\uparrow.
]

Once house prices increase, even relatively cautious households may face a choice between:

* borrowing more;
* purchasing a smaller or more distant property;
* postponing homeownership;
* remaining renters.

That creates a strategic or general-equilibrium element. A household's decision not to borrow does not prevent other households' borrowing from raising the market price it faces.

---

## 12. What the housing-credit literature supports

The empirical literature strongly supports a causal connection between credit supply and house prices.

Changes in conforming-loan eligibility that reduce mortgage-financing costs raise house prices. Mortgage disintermediation and the sale of originated loans were important components of the expansion in mortgage credit during the 2000s and were associated with house-price appreciation and subsequent defaults.

Expanded access to second mortgages was already being associated with reduced household saving by the late 1980s.

This evidence supports the model:

[
m_{it}
\leq
\bar \ell(\phi_t)P^H_t h_{it},
]

where (\bar\ell(\phi_t)) is an effective mortgage limit that increases with intermediation and securitization efficiency.

Housing supply should be modeled as

[
H^s_t
=====

\bar H(P^H_t)^\varepsilon_H.
]

When (\varepsilon_H) is low, a credit-supply expansion produces a larger price response and a smaller quantity response.

That yields the testable prediction:

[
\frac{\partial P^H}{\partial \phi}
\quad\text{is larger where housing supply is inelastic.}
]

---

## 13. Financialization should be modeled as risk transfer, not risk disappearance

Mian, Straub, and Sufi document a major rise in indirect asset ownership and in the length of financial-intermediation chains. The share of household financial assets held indirectly rose from around 45% in 1960 to approximately 70% before the financial crisis.

A lender that originates a mortgage can:

1. retain it;
2. sell it to a government-sponsored enterprise;
3. place it into a securitization structure;
4. transfer claims to funds, insurers, pensions, or households.

This can reduce the originating institution's direct exposure and make the claim more compatible with investors' portfolios.

But the model should include two countervailing effects:

[
\text{pooling}\Rightarrow
\text{less idiosyncratic concentration},
]

while

[
\text{common housing exposure}
+
\text{leverage}
+
\text{weaker monitoring}
\Rightarrow
\text{greater systemic vulnerability}.
]

The mechanism is therefore not “the investor can lose at most 5%.” It is that losses are more broadly distributed and individual exposures can be diversified, while aggregate tail risk remains.

---

## 14. Education debt is related but should not be in the baseline model

The intuition that easier education credit can raise both borrowing and prices is plausible, but the evidence is more mixed than for housing.

Expanded graduate-loan availability increased borrowing and prices in exposed programs without clear improvements in access or degree completion.

But other expansions in federal loan limits improved degree completion, later earnings, and repayment among credit-constrained students, without reducing homeownership or increasing other debt.

Student credit can therefore produce both:

[
\text{price pass-through or institutional rent capture}
]

and

[
\text{valuable human-capital investment}.
]

Housing and education should not be treated as identical durable-goods markets.

The recommended approach is:

* include housing in the baseline model;
* treat education as a separate extension;
* allow colleges to have capacity constraints or market power;
* measure human-capital returns rather than interpreting all education debt as consumption-related dissaving.

# Part V. Research Proposal A

## Global Scale, Creative Destruction, and Top Saving

### Research question

> **Does globalization increase the saving of the wealthy by raising the scalability, profits, and retained earnings of successful innovative firms?**

### Main mechanism

[
G_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{innovation prize}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{profit dispersion}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{business wealth concentration}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
S_{\text{top}}\uparrow.
]

The central contribution would be to distinguish three effects:

[
\text{capital gains},
]

[
\text{corporate retained earnings},
]

and

[
\text{personal saving out of entrepreneurial income}.
]

Only the latter two enter the active saving concept relevant to Mian, Straub, and Sufi.

### Minimal firm model

Each firm has product quality (q) and chooses R&D expenditure (x):

[
\pi(q,G)
========

G\cdot R(q)-wL-I-x.
]

Innovation arrives with probability

[
p(x)=1-e^{-\eta x}.
]

Entrants can displace incumbents when their quality exceeds the incumbent's quality.

Firm investment is financed through

[
I+x=n+b+e.
]

Debt is constrained by pledgeable assets:

[
b
\leq
\theta_T K^T+\theta_IK^I,
\qquad
\theta_I<\theta_T.
]

Profits can be distributed or retained:

[
\pi
===

Div+RE.
]

For a household owning share (\omega_i), attributable corporate saving is

[
S^{\pi}_{it}
============

\omega_i RE_t.
]

### Main hypotheses

**H1. Market-size effect**

Exogenous increases in foreign demand should raise innovation and retained earnings disproportionately among initially productive firms.

**H2. Concentration effect**

Industries with stronger global scalability should exhibit greater dispersion in firm growth, profits, and owner wealth.

**H3. Financing-composition effect**

As intangible intensity rises, successful innovative firms should rely more on equity, venture capital, and retained earnings relative to bank debt.

**H4. Saving rather than valuation**

The link to Mian, Straub, and Sufi should survive after excluding capital gains. A rise in market capitalization without a rise in retained earnings or unconsumed owner income would not explain their net-saving measure.

**H5. Ambiguous turnover**

The effect on entry and exit should vary by sector. Greater market scale can stimulate entry but can also allow dominant firms to acquire or deter entrants.

### Empirical design

A firm- or industry-level specification could be

[
Y_{jt}
======

\alpha_j+\delta_t
+
\beta_1 GlobalDemandShock_{jt}
+
\beta_2
\left(
GlobalDemandShock_{jt}
\times InitialProductivity_j
\right)
+
X_{jt}'\gamma
+\varepsilon_{jt}.
]

Outcomes should include:

* patenting and R&D;
* new-firm entry and exit;
* sales concentration;
* retained earnings;
* dividend payouts;
* debt and equity issuance;
* venture-capital funding;
* cash accumulation;
* intangible investment;
* merger and acquisition activity.

A foreign-demand shift-share shock can isolate demand growth in export destinations that is plausibly external to the firm's own decisions. Trade-policy and technology-cost shocks can provide complementary variation.

### Link to household saving

The difficult but important final step is to allocate changes in business income and retained earnings to household wealth groups.

Possible data sources include:

* DINA wealth and income categories;
* SCF business-ownership data;
* administrative pass-through-business income;
* corporate equity ownership by wealth group;
* national-accounts corporate saving;
* industry-level ownership mappings.

### Main limitation

This project is conceptually strong but empirically demanding. Linking exogenous firm-level opportunities to top-household active saving is much harder than showing that technology raises company valuations.

It is best viewed as the **firm-side paper** in a larger research agenda.

# Part VI. Research Proposal B

## Financialization, Housing Prices, and the Decline in Middle-Class Saving

### Research question

> **Did the expansion of financial intermediation and mortgage credit cause households with comparable real wealth to borrow more and save less after the early 1980s?**

### Main mechanism

[
\phi_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{mortgage supply}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
P^H_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
m_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
s_{\text{middle},t}\downarrow.
]

### Household problem

Households choose consumption (c), housing (h), mortgage debt (m), safe assets (a^s), and risky assets (a^r):

[
\max E_0
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}
\beta^t u(c_t,h_t).
]

Their budget constraint is

[
c_t
+
P^H_t(h_{t+1}-h_t)
+
a^s_{t+1}
+
a^r_{t+1}
+
R^m_tm_t
========

y_t
+
R^s_ta^s_t
+
R^r_ta^r_t
+
m_{t+1}.
]

Mortgage borrowing satisfies

[
m_{t+1}
\leq
\min
\left{
LTV_tP^H_th_{t+1},
,
DTI_ty_t
\right}.
]

Financialization affects the effective (LTV), interest rate, transaction cost, and probability of loan approval.

### Hypotheses

**H1. Credit-to-price effect**

Exogenous mortgage-credit expansions raise house prices, particularly where housing supply is inelastic.

**H2. Price-to-debt effect**

Later or younger buyers must take larger mortgages relative to income to purchase comparable housing.

**H3. Debt-to-saving effect**

Greater mortgage borrowing reduces active saving conditional on wealth and income.

**H4. Portfolio crowd-out**

Mortgage debt and housing concentration reduce participation in equities and other risky financial assets.

**H5. Distributional asymmetry**

Existing homeowners may receive capital gains, while first-time buyers and renters face higher entry prices. Therefore the same credit expansion has different effects across age, tenure, and initial wealth groups.

### Empirical design

A household specification could be

[
s_{ict}
=======

\alpha_c+\delta_t
+
\gamma CreditSupplyShock_{ct}
+
\eta
\left(
CreditSupplyShock_{ct}
\times LowSupplyElasticity_c
\right)
+
X_{ict}'\beta
+
\varepsilon_{ict}.
]

Potential outcomes include:

* mortgage origination;
* debt-to-income ratios;
* house prices;
* home-equity extraction;
* nonhousing consumption;
* liquid saving;
* stock-market participation;
* net saving conditional on wealth.

Potential sources of quasi-experimental variation include:

* changes in conforming-loan limits;
* lender exposure to securitization;
* banking deregulation;
* preexisting branch networks;
* differential bank exposure to the intangible-capital transition;
* mortgage-policy changes.

### Relationship to Mian, Straub, and Sufi

This proposal is a direct extension of the paper because the authors already establish:

[
\text{top saving}
\rightarrow
\text{middle-class debt}.
]

The extension would estimate the causal mechanism:

[
\text{financialization}
\rightarrow
\text{credit conditions}
\rightarrow
\text{house prices and leverage}
\rightarrow
\text{saving behavior}.
]

It is more immediately feasible than the full globalization project.

# Part VII. Recommended integrated proposal

## From Creative Destruction to Household Debt

### Core research question

> **Did the post-1980 transition toward a global, scalable, and intangible-intensive knowledge economy simultaneously increase top-end saving by concentrating entrepreneurial and corporate income, and reduce middle-class saving by redirecting financial intermediation away from business lending and toward mortgages and other household debt?**

This is the sharpest synthesis of the two ideas.

---

## 15. Integrated causal chain

### Stage 1: globalization and technological scalability

[
G_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{greater return to technological leadership}.
]

The best firms can serve a much larger market.

### Stage 2: concentration and corporate saving

[
\text{leader profits}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
RE_t\uparrow
\Rightarrow
S_{\text{top}}\uparrow.
]

Because business and corporate-equity ownership are concentrated, retained earnings accrue disproportionately to the wealthy. The paper explicitly counts retained corporate earnings as household saving and notes that corporate saving has become increasingly important for distributional saving.

### Stage 3: intangibility and financial segmentation

[
K^I/K\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{business collateral}\downarrow
\Rightarrow
\text{bank business lending}\downarrow.
]

Selected frontier firms receive venture or public equity, but many small firms remain constrained.

### Stage 4: bank and intermediary reallocation

[
L^{business}\downarrow
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
L^{mortgage}\uparrow.
]

Securitization makes mortgages particularly compatible with market-based investor demand.

### Stage 5: household leverage and prices

[
L^{mortgage}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
P^H\uparrow
\Rightarrow
D_{\text{middle}}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
S_{\text{middle}}\downarrow.
]

### Stage 6: portfolio feedback

[
D_{\text{middle}}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{liquid saving and risky participation}\downarrow,
]

while wealthy households continue holding business equity and diversified financial claims.

This produces a self-reinforcing distributional pattern:

[
\boxed{
S_{\text{top}}\uparrow,
\qquad
D_{\text{middle}}\uparrow,
\qquad
S_{\text{middle}}\downarrow,
\qquad
W_{\text{top}}/W_{\text{middle}}\uparrow.
}
]

---

# Part VIII. Structure of the integrated macro model

## 16. Agents

The model should contain four heterogeneous groups:

1. workers and potential homeowners;
2. potential entrepreneurs;
3. wealthy business owners and diversified investors;
4. financial intermediaries.

Households differ in:

[
W_i,\quad
y_i,\quad
z_i,\quad
\beta_i,\quad
\gamma_i,\quad
\kappa_i,
]

where (W_i) is wealth, (y_i) is labor income, (z_i) is entrepreneurial ability, (\beta_i) is patience, (\gamma_i) is risk aversion, and (\kappa_i) is the fixed cost of accessing sophisticated investments.

---

## 17. Firms

Potential entrepreneurs decide whether to enter:

[
V^{entry}(z_i,G_t,\lambda_t,\iota_t)
\geq
F_t.
]

Incumbents choose between:

* production;
* incremental innovation;
* radical innovation;
* acquisition of entrants;
* dividend payment;
* retained earnings.

The probability of displacement depends on entrant innovation and incumbent technology:

[
\delta_{it}
===========

\delta(q_{it},\lambda_t,x^{entrant}_t).
]

The model should allow a high-(\lambda) environment to have two competing effects:

[
\text{more opportunities to innovate}
]

versus

[
\text{shorter duration of innovation rents}.
]

Therefore the effect of faster technology on entry and investment is theoretically ambiguous.

---

## 18. Financial institutions

Banks allocate funding between business and mortgage loans:

[
L^F_t+L^H_t=D_t+B_t.
]

Their expected returns depend on collateral, screening, default, capital requirements, and securitization.

A rise in intangible production lowers recovery values on firm loans:

[
Recovery^F_t
============

\theta_TK^T_t+\theta_IK^I_t,
\qquad
\theta_I<\theta_T.
]

A rise in securitization efficiency increases the marketability of mortgages:

[
Liquidity^H_t
=============

\ell(\phi_t),
\qquad
\ell'(\phi)>0.
]

Banks therefore have an endogenous incentive to shift from (L^F) toward (L^H).

---

## 19. Investor portfolios

Wealthy households choose between:

[
a^{safe},
\quad
a^{public\ equity},
\quad
a^{private\ equity},
\quad
a^{venture},
\quad
a^{mortgage\ claims},
\quad
a^{government}.
]

Access to private or specialized investments requires a fixed cost:

[
\kappa(W),
\qquad
\kappa'(W)<0.
]

This generates endogenous segmentation: wealthy investors can access a broader opportunity set, while middle-wealth households concentrate in housing, pensions, deposits, and public-market funds.

## 20. Saving concept

The model must reproduce the paper's net-saving concept:

[
s^N_{it}
========

\frac{\Theta_{it}}
{Z^{d,p}*{it}+Z^{d,\pi}*{it}},
]

where (Z^{d,\pi}_{it}) includes corporate disposable income attributable to the household.

Capital gains must be separately recorded:

[
\Delta W_{it}
=============

\Theta_{it}
+
\pi^A_tW_{i,t-1}.
]

This distinction generates a decisive falsification test.

A technology shock that only raises

[
\pi^A_tW_{i,t-1}
]

but not

[
\Theta_{it}
]

can explain wealth inequality but not the net saving-rate increase documented by Mian, Straub, and Sufi.

# Part IX. Falsifiable predictions

The integrated model should produce the following predictions.

### Prediction 1

Exogenous foreign-demand expansions increase innovation, retained earnings, and owner income disproportionately in initially productive firms.

### Prediction 2

The effect of globalization on top active saving is larger in industries with concentrated household equity ownership and high retained earnings.

### Prediction 3

Greater intangible intensity reduces the use of conventional bank debt among innovative firms and increases reliance on equity, venture capital, or internal funds.

### Prediction 4

Banks more exposed to the intangible-capital transition shift relatively more lending toward mortgages.

### Prediction 5

Counties served by those banks experience larger increases in mortgage originations and house prices, especially where housing supply is inelastic.

### Prediction 6

Households exposed to larger mortgage-credit shocks show lower saving conditional on real wealth, income, age, and initial homeownership.

### Prediction 7

Mortgage leverage reduces liquid financial saving and risky-asset participation.

### Prediction 8

The effects of technology on firm entry and exit vary by industry and financing conditions rather than producing an unconditional aggregate rise in turnover.

### Prediction 9

The top 1% increasingly hold both:

[
\text{business and corporate equity}
]

and

[
\text{intermediated claims on household debt}.
]

### Prediction 10

Recomputing productive investment with broader intangible-capital measures reduces—but need not eliminate—the gap between top saving and measured investment.

Some complementary investments associated with general-purpose technologies are poorly measured, and broader organizational or marketing assets remain imperfectly captured even though R&D and software are already capitalized in U.S. national accounts.

## Phase 1: complete the distributional measurement

* retained earnings;

Do not use patents alone as the measure of technology pace. Combine patent measures with product turnover, technology-related hiring, R&D, intangible investment, and firm outcomes.

# Part XI. Which project is most robust?

## Most robust standalone empirical proposal

The strongest standalone extension of Mian, Straub, and Sufi is:

> **Did the transition toward intangible production cause banks to reallocate credit from business lending toward mortgages, thereby helping transform the saving glut of the rich into middle-class household debt?**

This is more precise and empirically defensible than the broad claim that faster technological change caused more firms to enter and die.

It has four advantages:

1. It is directly connected to the paper's tracing of top saving into middle-class debt.
2. It uses an established empirical relationship between intangible capital and bank portfolio reallocation.
3. It explains why an economy with major technological opportunities may nevertheless direct excess saving toward housing rather than productive investment.
4. It generates measurable bank-, county-, and household-level predictions.

## Most ambitious theoretical proposal

The more ambitious model is:

> **How do globalization, creative destruction, and intangible capital jointly determine entrepreneurial wealth, top saving, financial intermediation, house prices, and middle-class leverage?**

This is potentially more novel, but it contains many mechanisms and should probably be developed after establishing the empirical bridge.

## Weakest version to avoid

The following formulation should not be used as the central hypothesis:

> “Technology has changed faster since 1982, causing more firms to be born and die, and these firms mainly borrow from rich households.”

It is too broad because:

* aggregate U.S. dynamism has declined;
* technological effects differ across industries;
* many frontier firms use equity rather than conventional debt;
* intermediaries and institutional investors complicate direct rich-to-startup lending;
* higher valuations alone are not active saving;
* much of the paper's observed top saving ultimately finances household and government debt.

# Part XII. Policy implications

## Innovation and competition policy

If global scalability produces concentrated rents, policy must balance:

[
\text{innovation incentives}
]

against

[
\text{entry barriers and persistent market power}.
]

Merger review, platform access, interoperability, and startup-acquisition policy may affect both creative destruction and the distribution of entrepreneurial returns.

## Financing intangible firms

If innovative firms are credit constrained because intangible assets are difficult to pledge, possible responses include:

* public R&D grants;
* innovation guarantees;
* intellectual-property-backed finance;
* better venture-market access;
* public co-investment;
* policies supporting competitive equity markets.

The objective would be to channel more of the saving surplus toward productive experimentation without socializing all entrepreneurial losses.

## Mortgage and macroprudential policy

If financialization redirects capital toward housing and amplifies price-leverage feedbacks, relevant instruments include:

* loan-to-value and debt-to-income limits;
* underwriting and screening requirements;
* intermediary capital requirements;
* regulation of securitization incentives;
* stress testing of common housing exposures;
* consumer mortgage protections.

## Housing supply

Credit regulation alone may not solve affordability if housing supply is structurally inelastic.

Land-use and construction policies can reduce

[
\frac{\partial P^H}{\partial CreditSupply},
]

thereby weakening the feedback from easier credit to higher prices and larger required mortgages.

## Education finance

Student-credit policy should distinguish valuable human-capital investment from institutional price capture.

Possible tools include:

* income-contingent repayment;
* grants targeted at constrained students;
* program-level accountability;
* limits linked to expected outcomes;
* increased educational supply and capacity.

## Portfolio access

Broader access to diversified equity can potentially allow middle-income households to share more directly in technological returns. But expanding access should not be interpreted as eliminating investment risk, and it does not substitute for reducing excessive household leverage.

## Tax and corporate-saving policy

If retained corporate earnings explain part of top saving, the tax treatment of retained versus distributed earnings, realization, and business ownership becomes relevant.

The policy question is not merely whether rich households save too much. It is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Why does the financial system direct their surplus saving toward some uses rather than others?}
}
]

# Recommended proposal title

## **From Creative Destruction to Household Debt: Global Scalability, Intangible Capital, and the Saving Glut of the Rich**

---

# Ready-to-use proposal abstract

Mian, Straub, and Sufi document that the post-1980 saving glut of the U.S. top 1% financed a substantial expansion of middle-class household debt before 2008 and federal debt thereafter, rather than a comparable increase in productive investment. This project develops a micro-founded macroeconomic explanation linking that result to the transition toward a global and intangible-intensive knowledge economy. Greater global market scale increases the reward to successful innovation, concentrating profits, business wealth, and retained corporate earnings among wealthy firm owners. At the same time, the growing importance of intangible capital reduces the collateral value of business assets, leading innovative firms to rely disproportionately on equity, venture capital, and internal funds while inducing traditional intermediaries to reallocate lending toward mortgages and other collateralized household credit. Financial innovation and securitization make these household liabilities increasingly compatible with the portfolio demand of wealthy savers. The resulting expansion of mortgage credit raises housing prices and middle-class leverage, lowers saving conditional on wealth, and can crowd out participation in risky financial assets. The project first tests the firm, bank, and household components of this mechanism using variation in global demand, intangible-capital exposure, mortgage-market structure, and local housing supply. It then embeds the estimated mechanisms in a heterogeneous-agent model disciplined by the distributional saving and unveiled debt moments in Mian, Straub, and Sufi. The framework quantifies how much of the saving-rate divergence can be attributed to global scalability, intangible production, financial intermediation, and housing-market feedbacks.
